\label{subsec:results-1}

Figure~\ref{fig:curves} shows the learning curves, each averaged over its seeds. The left panel
is labelled \emph{training objective} rather than loss because the ablation arm minimises
Equation~\ref{eq:dice} and the others Equation~\ref{eq:cross-entropy}; the two are not on a
common scale and should be read only against their own arm.

    \caption{Learning curves; solid validation, dashed training, band one standard deviation.}
    \label{fig:curves}

\textbf{The budget was the right size, for three of the five arms.} The pretrained DeepLabV3 runs
stopped at epochs 38, 39 and 40 with their best epoch at 30, 31 and 35, and their validation
curve is flat from about epoch 32, rising from $0.615$ to $0.616$ over the last eight epochs. The
40-epoch budget was therefore adequate without being generous. The ResNet50 run behaved the same
way, best at epoch 37 of 40.

\textbf{The U-Net is the exception, and its number is a lower bound.} Its best epochs were 38, 40
and 39 of 40, and its validation mIoU rises monotonically to the last epoch, $0.446$ at epoch 28,
$0.460$ at 32, $0.466$ at 36 and $0.468$ at 40. It was still improving when the budget ran out.
Every U-Net figure in this report is therefore a floor rather than a converged score, which
Section~\ref{subsec:results-7} returns to.

\textbf{Overfitting is present but mild, and is not what separates the arms.}
Table~\ref{tab:behaviour} gives the gap between training and validation mIoU at the selected
epoch. The pretrained arms sit between $0.11$ and $0.16$, and validation mIoU never turns
downward for them; the U-Net's gap is only $0.021$, which is consistent with a model still
underfitting at epoch 40 rather than one generalising unusually well. The Dice arm is the
instructive case: its gap is $-0.002$, so it does not overfit at all, it simply stops improving.

\textbf{Early stopping.} It fired for three arms and not for the U-Net or ResNet50. The frozen
arm stopped at 23, 32 and 40 epochs, a spread of 17 epochs from nothing but the seed, which is a
reminder that the stopping epoch is a far noisier quantity than the score it protects. The Dice
arm stopped at epoch 12 on all three seeds, having peaked at epoch 4 on all three;
Section~\ref{subsec:results-6} explains why.

\textbf{Training time.} The 13 runs took $25.5$ hours in total on the single machine of
Table~\ref{tab:protocol}, run consecutively so that the timings compare with each other, from
$0.50$ hours for a Dice run that stopped at epoch 12 to $8.22$ hours for ResNet50.

\textbf{Implementation issues found and fixed.} Four, each of which would have corrupted a
reported number. The qualitative-example selection kept only the first six images of the split
before ranking them, so the ``failure case'' was the worst of an arbitrary six rather than of the
split; after the fix the selected range widened from $0.58$ down to $0.42$, and after it from $0.77$ down to $0.29$ image mIoU.
The selected examples were then discarded before any overlay was written, so the figures could
never have been produced. Inference latency was measured with autograd active, which is not what
inference does; the measured effect on this backend was within noise, $14.82$ against
$15.71$~ms, but it would dominate peak memory on CUDA. And two early draft runs used ten epochs:
because the cosine period equals the configured epoch count, their learning-rate trajectory
differs from a 40-epoch run at every step, so they were deleted rather than pooled.

Three observations follow from the two tables before any comparison is drawn.

\textbf{Pixel accuracy and mIoU disagree, exactly as the EDA predicted.} In
Table~\ref{tab:secondary} the U-Net reaches $0.9202$ pixel accuracy, higher than both the frozen
arm ($0.8959$) and the Dice arm ($0.8982$), while scoring lower than both on mIoU. A metric
dominated by the three classes that cover $75.8\%$ of the ground ranks these three models in the
opposite order to the metric that weights all nineteen equally. This is the measured
justification for the choice made in Section~\ref{subsec:problem-and-data-3}, and it is why
pixel accuracy appears in this report only as a secondary column.

\textbf{Validation does not overstate test here; for four arms of five it understates it.} Test
mIoU exceeds validation mIoU by $0.0043$ for DeepLabV3, $0.0042$ for the frozen arm, $0.0024$ for
Dice and $0.0412$ for ResNet50. Only the U-Net is lower on test, by $0.0134$. This runs against
the usual expectation and has a simple cause: with a city-level split, the three test cities are
simply a different sample of difficulty from the three validation cities, and that
partition-to-partition difference is of the same order as, or larger than, the seed-to-seed
spread. Pixel accuracy moves the other way for every arm, falling on test while mIoU rises, which
is consistent with the test cities holding a class mix that is harder per pixel but better
populated in the rarer classes. What transfers between the two columns is therefore the margin
between two arms rather than the absolute level.

\textbf{The seed spread is small except for the U-Net.} The standard deviation across seeds is
$0.0049$ to $0.0088$ validation mIoU for the three pretrained arms but $0.0154$ for the U-Net,
three times larger, with individual seeds at $0.4687$, $0.4824$ and $0.4995$. Training from
scratch is markedly less reproducible than fine-tuning here. The U-Net's best seed alone would
have been indistinguishable from the frozen arm; its worst would have lost under the rule. A
single-seed comparison could therefore have reported either outcome, which is the concrete
argument for the three-seed protocol.

The three rows say different things. In the correct case the large regions are recovered almost
exactly and the visible error is a thin outline tracing every boundary: the model knows what each
region is and is imprecise about where it ends. The difficult case adds the failure mode the
confusion matrix quantifies, with poles and signs partially absorbed into the building behind
them.

The failure case is the most informative, and not for the reason its mIoU suggests. Its error
panel reports that only $5.8\%$ of labelled pixels are wrong, a \emph{lower} error rate than the
split average of $6.7\%$, yet it is the worst image in the split at $0.204$ mIoU. The cause is
visible in the ground truth: most of the courtyard carries the ignore label, so little of the
frame is scored, and the few classes that are present have small support. A per-image mean
over classes is extremely sensitive when each class is represented by a handful of pixels. The
lesson is that per-image mIoU and per-image pixel error are only loosely coupled, and that
``worst image by mIoU'' selects for images with little labelled content as much as for images the
model misunderstands.

\textbf{The headline is a single large effect, and three ways of losing it.} One configuration,
the fully fine-tuned pretrained model, separates from everything else. The other three
three-seed arms are mutually indistinguishable, clustered within $0.0126$ validation mIoU of one
another despite differing in architecture, in pretraining, and in objective. Freezing the
backbone, swapping cross-entropy for Dice, and training a different architecture from scratch are
three unrelated interventions, and all three land in the same place. The frozen arm and the Dice
arm differ by $0.0003$. This is the strongest result in the report: on this dataset at this
budget, what matters is not which of several reasonable choices is made but whether the model
starts from adapted pretrained features at all.

\textbf{Where the difference appears.} Figure~\ref{fig:per-class} breaks the aggregate down.
Against the frozen arm, full fine-tuning is better on \emph{all nineteen} classes with
non-overlapping intervals, so nothing about the frozen arm is preserved. But the gap is very
unevenly distributed: $0.027$ on road, $0.044$ on car and $0.048$ on building, against $0.215$ on
truck, $0.213$ on traffic sign, $0.173$ on traffic light and $0.166$ on bus. A frozen
object-centric backbone still supports the classes that fill a street scene and loses precisely
the classes that do not.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/per_class_iou_test.pdf}
    \caption{Per-class IoU on test, hardest class first.}
    \label{fig:per-class}
\end{figure}

This partly supports the hypothesis of the controlled experiment and partly refutes it. The prediction that the gap would
be widest on thin classes is right for traffic sign and traffic light, which are second and
third largest, but the single widest gap is truck, a rare-but-large class, and pole, the thin
class named explicitly in the hypothesis, sits mid-table at $0.128$. The correct statement is
narrower than the one pre-registered: freezing costs most on the classes that are \emph{rare in
the training distribution}, whether they are rare because they are thin in area or because they
are seldom photographed, and costs least on the three classes that cover most of the ground.

\textbf{Pretraining is not uniformly better, which the aggregate hides.} The U-Net beats the
pretrained DeepLabV3, with non-overlapping intervals, on three classes: pole
($0.4126$ against $0.3277$), sky ($0.9332$ against $0.9211$) and vegetation ($0.8823$ against
$0.8740$). Pole is the interesting one, a $26\%$ relative margin for the weaker model. The cause
is architectural rather than about features. DeepLabV3 never resolves below a stride of $8$ and
returns its logits to full resolution by one bilinear interpolation, which cannot reconstruct a
structure a few pixels wide; the U-Net's skip connections carry full-resolution detail into the
decoder, which is exactly what a pole needs. The from-scratch baseline is better at localisation
and far worse at recognition, and mIoU averages the two into a single number that shows only the
second. The per-class table therefore admits no unqualified claim that the pretrained model is
the better one.

\textbf{Compute cost.} Table~\ref{tab:results} makes the cost comparison unusually clean, because
the best accuracy-per-cost model is also the most accurate of the three-seed arms. The U-Net is
dominated on every axis simultaneously: it holds $30\%$ fewer parameters than DeepLabV3 yet takes
$1.9$ times as long per epoch, $1.5$ times as long to serve one image, $2.7$ times as long at
batch 8, and $1.9$ times the peak memory, while scoring $0.130$ lower. Parameter count is simply
not a proxy for cost here: the U-Net's convolutions run at full resolution and its decoder holds
every encoder map alive for the skip connections, whereas MobileNetV3 reduces resolution early
and keeps it reduced. The frozen arm is the one genuine trade: it trains in $0.84$ hours against
$1.52$, a $45\%$ saving, for $0.118$ mIoU. Note that this saving is smaller than ``freezing the
backbone'' suggests, because $73\%$ of the parameters remain trainable, and that it is a saving in
\emph{training} only: inference cost is identical, $14.7$ against $15.2$~ms, since the same
forward pass runs either way.

\textbf{Scaling the backbone works, and is expensive.} ResNet50 reaches $0.6665$ validation and
$0.7076$ test mIoU, against MobileNetV3's $0.6136$ and $0.6180$. At one seed this supports no
claim of superiority under the decision rule, and it is reported as indicative. What can be said
is weaker but not empty: the margin, $0.0529$ on validation, is ten times the seed-to-seed
standard deviation measured for the same architecture at the same protocol, so it is unlikely to
be seed noise alone. The cost is substantial: $5.3$ times the seconds per epoch, $4.8$ times the
single-image latency, $9.4$ times at batch 8, and $3.6$ times the parameters, for roughly a
$9\%$ relative gain in test mIoU. On this hardware a single ResNet50 run costs more than the nine
MobileNetV3 runs of the other three experiments combined.

\textbf{Hypotheses.} The model comparison is supported, with the per-class qualification above.
The controlled experiment is supported on its main claim and refuted on its per-class prediction.
The ablation is refuted outright, as Section~\ref{subsec:results-6} sets out. The backbone
extension is consistent with its hypothesis but cannot establish it at one seed.

\textbf{What does not follow.} Three things. The U-Net's $0.4836$ is a floor, not a converged
score, so ``pretraining beats from-scratch by $0.130$'' is an upper bound on that margin at this
budget rather than an estimate of it. The comparison attributes that gap to pretraining, but
the two arms differ in architecture as well, and no run here separates a DeepLabV3 trained from
scratch from a pretrained one; that single experiment would isolate the factor properly and was
not in the budget. And every number is a score at an untuned configuration under one shared
protocol, so none of them is the best the corresponding method could reach.

\subsection{Error analysis}
\label{subsec:results-5}

\textbf{The structure of the errors is a property of the task, not of the model.} The five
configurations differ
by more than a factor of two in error rate, from $4.9\%$ to $10.4\%$, yet their error
\emph{composition} is nearly identical: thin structures take between $19$ and $25\%$ for all
five, upright surfaces between $23$ and $27\%$, ground surfaces between $15$ and $20\%$. Improving the model lowers the total and
leaves the proportions where they were. There is one exception, treated below.

\textbf{Thin structures, $24.7\%$.} Any error where a pole, traffic light or traffic sign is on
either side. The largest single flows in the whole matrix sit here: pole predicted as building,
$0.91$M pixels or $5.9\%$ of all errors, pole as vegetation, $0.56$M or $3.6\%$, and traffic sign
as building, $0.28$M. Pole is the hardest class of the nineteen at $0.3230$ IoU despite appearing in
$99\%$ of images, so this is not a data-scarcity failure: it is a resolution failure, and
Figure~\ref{fig:examples} shows it directly. The cause is the one identified in
Section~\ref{subsec:results-4}: a stride-8 feature map upsampled bilinearly cannot represent a
structure a few pixels wide, and a pole is mostly boundary. The evidence that this is
architectural rather than inherent is that the from-scratch U-Net, with its full-resolution skip
connections, scores $0.4361$ on pole against DeepLabV3's $0.3230$ while losing on almost
everything else.

\textbf{Upright surfaces, $24.0\%$.} Confusions among building, wall, fence and vegetation, led
by building predicted as vegetation, $1.16$M pixels, and vegetation as building, $1.13$M, which
are nearly symmetric. These are overwhelmingly boundary pixels where a tree overhangs a facade,
and the near-symmetry is the signature of a boundary error rather than a systematic bias. Wall
and fence add a second cause: both are low vertical barriers that the taxonomy separates from
building by a convention rather than an appearance, and both are among the weakest classes at
$0.4345$ and $0.4689$.

\textbf{Ground surfaces, $14.6\%$.} Road, sidewalk and terrain confused with one another,
$1.12$M pixels of sidewalk predicted as road and $0.74$M the reverse. The boundary between road
and sidewalk is a kerb a few pixels high, often occluded by parked cars, and at $512 \times 1024$
it is sometimes not visible at all. This group grows to $20.3\%$ for the frozen arm, the largest
compositional shift in the table.

\textbf{Traffic participants, $6.5\%$, and the one place a model differs in kind.} Confusions
among the eight vehicle and person classes. This is the smallest group for every DeepLabV3 arm at
between $6.3$ and $6.8\%$, and it is $12.5\%$ for the U-Net, nearly double. The cause is visible in the
per-class table: the U-Net scores $0.0205$ on rider, against DeepLabV3's $0.4316$, and $0.0108$
on motorcycle. Rider and person differ not in appearance but in what the person is sitting on, a
distinction that requires recognising the vehicle beneath them, and the from-scratch model never
learns it. This is the one error group where the deficit is a failure of recognition rather than
of localisation, and it is where pretrained features help most.

\textbf{Participant versus background, $17.5\%$, and other, $12.7\%$.} The first is a person or
vehicle confused with scenery, led by person predicted as building, $0.22$M, and road predicted
as car, $0.21$M; these are object boundaries and shadow regions. The remainder is thinly spread
and dominated by vegetation against terrain, $0.25$M, and building against sky, $0.22$M, both
again boundary pairs.

\textbf{What would improve it.} Five of the six groups are dominated by boundary pixels, which
points at resolution and not at the objective or the data. Three ideas follow, in descending
order of expected return. Evaluating at the native $1024 \times 2048$ rather than the resized
$512 \times 1024$ would halve the boundary length measured in pixels, and the resize is a choice
made for compute, not for accuracy. An architecture that keeps a full-resolution path, which the
U-Net's advantage on pole suggests concretely, would address the thin-structure group that is the
single largest; a DeepLabV3+ decoder is the standard form of this. Only the traffic-participant
group would benefit from more or better data rather than more resolution, and it is the smallest.
What is \emph{not} indicated is a reweighted loss: Section~\ref{subsec:results-6} measures that
directly and it makes matters worse.

\subsection{Ablation study}
\label{subsec:results-6}
% Required: Mandatory for Assignments 2 and 3; recommended for Assignment 1 when comparing representations or regularizers.
% Also required: Section 21: at least one ablation is mandatory for Assignment 2.

The ablation replaces cross-entropy with the soft Dice loss of Equation~\ref{eq:dice}, holding
the model, the data and every other protocol value fixed. The hypothesis, fixed in advance, was
that Dice would raise IoU on train, bus and truck, the classes that are rare but large when
present, because it weights classes rather than pixels; and that it would not help pole or
traffic sign, whose errors are boundaries. The pre-registered criterion was that the hypothesis
holds only if the per-class rule separates the two arms on at least two of train, bus and truck.

\textbf{The hypothesis is refuted, and in the opposite direction.}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} applies the rule. The two arms are separated on train and on truck, so
the criterion is met in form, but Dice is \emph{worse} on both, and worse on every other class
tested. Dice is worse overall, $0.4958 \pm 0.0045$ against $0.6136 \pm 0.0051$ validation mIoU,
and worse on all nineteen classes individually.

\textbf{Why, and the mechanism is measurable.} The last column of Table~\ref{tab:ablation} is
the key. Across all nineteen classes the correlation between the probability that a class is
absent from a training batch and the damage Dice does to that class is $r = -0.809$: the more
often a class is missing from the batch, the more Dice costs it. The four worst-damaged classes
are exactly the four most often absent.

The cause lies in how the two objectives treat a class with no positive pixels in the batch.
Under cross-entropy such a class contributes nothing of its own; it is only ever a competitor in
the softmax denominator. Under Equation~\ref{eq:dice} it contributes a full term,
$(0 + \varepsilon) / (\sum_\Omega p_{cij} + 0 + \varepsilon)$, which is maximised by driving the
predicted probability of that class to zero everywhere in the batch. Dice therefore \emph{actively
suppresses} every class that happens to be missing from the current eight crops. Measured over
sampled batches, $10.43$ of the $19$ classes appear in a given $512 \times 512$ crop and $15.92$
in a batch of eight, so $16.2\%$ of the Dice terms in a typical step are suppression terms. For
train, which appears in $2\%$ of crops, roughly $85\%$ of the $301$ steps in an epoch push its
logit down and about $45$ steps push it up. The result is the only exact zero in this report:
under Dice the model never predicts a single train pixel, on any seed, on either split.

\textbf{This also explains the training behaviour.} The Dice arm peaked at epoch $4$ on all three
seeds and early stopping ended it at epoch $12$, with a training-to-validation gap of $-0.002$.
It is not overfitting and it is not underfitting in the usual sense: the objective reaches a
state where the suppression terms balance the overlap terms and stops producing useful gradient.
The Dice arm is also the only one that is \emph{more} expensive per epoch than its
cross-entropy counterpart, $150.5$ against $140.4$ seconds, since it computes a softmax and
per-class sums; it is cheapest overall only because it quits early.

\textbf{What this does and does not establish.} It establishes that an unweighted multi-class
soft Dice with $\varepsilon = 1$, computed per batch at batch size 8, is the wrong objective for
this class distribution, and it identifies the reason precisely enough to act on. It does not
establish that class-balanced objectives are useless here: the natural follow-ups, accumulating
Dice over an epoch rather than a batch, masking absent classes out of the mean, or a
class-weighted cross-entropy that adds no suppression term, all avoid the specific mechanism
measured above and none of them was run. The ablation revises the direction of
Section~\ref{subsec:results-5}'s recommendations rather than supporting them: the remedy for
rare classes is not to reweight the loss as implemented here.

\subsection{Limitations and conclusion}
\label{subsec:results-7}
% Required: Data, method and experimental limits; generalization; future work; short answer to the research question.

Six limits bound these results. \emph{The U-Net is undertrained:} its validation mIoU was still
rising at epoch 40 on all three seeds, so $0.4836$ is a floor and the margin over it an upper
bound. \emph{The model comparison confounds two factors:} its arms differ in architecture as
well as in pretraining, so the gap is attributable to the pair, not to pretraining alone.
\emph{The backbone extension is one seed} and supports no claim of superiority. \emph{Three seeds estimate a standard deviation from
three observations,} so ``indistinguishable'' means not separated at this sample size, not equal.
\emph{The data is narrow:} daylight, fair weather, European cities and one camera, and the images
were resized to a quarter of their pixels for compute, which is itself a ceiling on the thin
classes that dominate the error analysis. \emph{And reproducibility has a hardware limit:} MPS
provides no deterministic kernel for the interpolation DeepLabV3 uses, so rerunning a DeepLabV3
seed will not reproduce its number exactly, and the reported spread includes that
nondeterminism.

The research question was how much of the gap between a from-scratch model and a pretrained one
comes from pretrained features rather than from architecture, loss or capacity. The evidence
gives a sharp answer and an unexpected qualification. Fully fine-tuned pretrained features are
worth $0.1301$ validation mIoU over the from-scratch baseline, $0.1175$ over the same weights
with the backbone frozen, and $0.1178$ over the same model trained with a class-balanced
objective; and those three alternatives are mutually indistinguishable, within $0.0126$ of one
another. One factor accounts for essentially the whole spread. The qualification is that the
advantage is not uniform: the from-scratch U-Net is better, with separated intervals, on pole,
sky and vegetation, because a full-resolution decoder localises thin structures that a stride-8
backbone cannot, and five of the six error groups are dominated by boundary pixels. Pretraining
supplies recognition rather than resolution.

DeepLabV3-MobileNetV3, fully fine-tuned, is carried forward at
$\mathbf{0.6180 \pm 0.0049}$ test mIoU and $0.7441 \pm 0.0048$ test Dice, at $140$ seconds per
epoch and $15.2$~ms per image.

Three directions follow, in the order the evidence supports them. The first is resolution:
evaluating and training at the native $1024 \times 2048$, or adding a decoder with a
full-resolution path, attacks the thin-structure and boundary groups that are most of the error.
The second is the clean version of the model comparison, a DeepLabV3 trained from scratch,
which would separate pretraining from architecture in one run. The third is the class-balancing question the ablation
reopened rather than closed: an epoch-accumulated Dice, or a Dice that masks absent classes out
of its mean, avoids the suppression mechanism measured here and is the obvious next thing to try.
