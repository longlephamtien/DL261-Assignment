\small
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Almost two thirds of all errors sit in one four-class block}, and every confusion
          inside it is bidirectional (Table~\ref{tab:error-groups}).
    \item Largest flows: Shirt $\to$ T-shirt/top 118 of 1{,}000 Shirts, T-shirt/top $\to$ Shirt 78,
          Shirt $\to$ Coat 75.
    \item \textbf{Shirt is the floor for all five models}: F1 0.7365, precision 0.753, recall 0.721.
          It is the only class below 0.80.
\end{itemize}

%----------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Causes and Improvements}
\small
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The cause is in the data, not the model.} These four classes differ by sleeve length,
          collar shape and fabric texture, cues that survive $28\times28$ grayscale poorly. The EDA
          predicted it: they have the most similar class means and dominate a nearest-class-mean
          classifier before any network is trained.
    \item \textbf{It is not architectural.} The same block holds 64\% of the MLP's errors, 64\% of the
          GRU's, 61\% of the Transformer's and 48\% of the baseline's. The baseline is the exception
          that confirms the rule: its errors are least concentrated only because it makes 2{,}369 of them.
    \item \textbf{Footwear is not worth attacking:} sole shape and ankle height survive downsampling,
          so all three exceed 0.95 F1 on only 93 errors.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{2pt}
\begin{block}{Capacity is demonstrably not the fix}
\small
The Transformer carries 28\% more parameters than the CNN and a \emph{worse} Shirt F1. Three
directions match the diagnosis instead: a class-weighted or focal loss trading precision on the
easier classes for Shirt recall; augmentation targeted at sleeve and collar geometry rather than a
generic crop; or a second-stage classifier specialising on the four-class block.
\end{block}
\end{frame}
