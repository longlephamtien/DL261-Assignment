## Problem statement

Referring-expression segmentation: given a photograph and a natural-language expression naming one
object in it, predict the mask of that object alone. The prediction unit is one (image, expression)
pair rather than one image, so a single photograph yields several independent predictions with
different targets.

What makes the task multimodal rather than segmentation with text attached is that neither modality
settles it. Every referred object shares its category with other instances in the same frame, so a
model that recognises categories but cannot read the expression has no basis for choosing among
them. The expression on its own is equally insufficient, since it names a position relative to
other objects without saying where either lies in pixels.

Four models are compared under one split and one evaluation protocol: image only, text only, a
simple late fusion, and an improved fusion in which visual positions attend over the tokens. The
question is how much of the task each modality determines by itself, and whether the way the two
are joined changes the answer.

## Dataset and EDA

RefCOCO, UNC partition: $142{,}210$ referring expressions over $50{,}000$ annotated objects in
$19{,}994$ MS COCO 2014 photographs. The expressions were collected in a two-player game where one
player described an object and the other had to click it; expressions that failed were discarded,
so what survives is language that succeeded at separating one instance from its neighbours.

Every pair carries all three of its parts. Expression, image and mask are missing in $0$ of the
$50{,}000$ objects, which removes the missing-modality case the handbook asks about: there is no
policy to state because there is nothing to handle.

The annotations carry no licence of their own, while the images are licensed individually under
seven Creative Commons terms declared in the COCO metadata. $13{,}904$ of the $19{,}994$ images,
$69.5\%$, carry a NonCommercial clause and $6{,}353$ carry NoDerivs or ShareAlike. Educational use
is covered by all seven and redistribution is not, so the images are fetched from COCO on demand
and kept out of version control, leaving only the split file and the measured statistics committed.

**Neither modality is sufficient, and the data fixes by how much.** A referred object shares its
category with $4.90$ other instances on average and never stands alone: not one of the $50{,}000$
objects is the sole instance of its category in its frame. A model that recognises the right
category but cannot read the expression therefore picks the intended instance $30.5\%$ of the time
under uniform guessing, which is the ceiling the image-only baseline works against. That gap is
what fusion has to close, and it is a property of the data rather than an assumption of the design.

:::figure{label="fig:a3-distractors"}
![Two panels. Left, a histogram of same-category instances per referred object, peaking at two and falling away to a maximum of twenty-two. Right, the chance of guessing the target against the instance count, a reciprocal curve crossing a dashed line at 30.5 percent](/DL261-Assignment/assignments/assignment-3/distractors.svg)

Same-category instances per target, and what an image-only model can reach without the expression.
:::

**Expressions are short and largely spatial.** The mean is $3.50$ tokens and the median $3$;
$36.8\%$ are at most two tokens and the longest is $39$. Over $497{,}738$ tokens the vocabulary is
$10{,}341$ word types. $46.3\%$ of expressions contain a direction word, and the two most frequent
words in the corpus name a side. Those words are reliable: they agree with the side the target
actually occupies $96.2\%$ of the time.

Two design consequences follow. A horizontal flip must rewrite the expression it flips, since
leaving the text alone would mislabel nearly half the training data; the loader therefore maps left
to right, leftmost to rightmost and back. And at this length the cost of attending over the tokens
is small beside the vision branch, which is what makes the cross-attention model affordable.

:::figure{label="fig:a3-expressions"}
![Three panels: a histogram of expression length concentrated between one and six tokens, a bar chart of the most frequent words with left and right at the top, and the share of expressions containing a spatial word](/DL261-Assignment/assignments/assignment-3/expressions.svg)

Expression length, the most frequent words, and the share that is spatial.
:::

**Targets are large and half of them are people.** The median target covers $8.3\%$ of its frame
and none covers under $1\%$, so the small-object regime that dominates ordinary instance
segmentation does not arise here. Across $78$ distinct categories, person accounts for $24{,}767$ of
the $50{,}000$ objects.

:::figure{label="fig:a3-targets"}
![Two panels: a distribution of target area as a share of the frame, centred near eight percent, and a bar chart of the most frequent categories with person far ahead of elephant, zebra and chair](/DL261-Assignment/assignments/assignment-3/targets.svg)

Target size and category frequency.
:::

**The split unit is the image.** Every expression and every object belonging to one photograph sits
in one partition, so no frame is seen on both sides of a comparison. Measured rather than assumed:
the image sets of all six partition pairs are disjoint, with zero overlap.

The two test splits are opposites by construction: testA is $93.0\%$ people and testB contains none
at all, against roughly half in train and validation. Pooling them would average over that
difference and hide it, so they are reported separately throughout.
