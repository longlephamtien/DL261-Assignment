## Problem statement

Semantic segmentation of urban street scenes: every pixel of a road-level photograph is assigned one of nineteen classes, from road and building down to traffic light and motorcycle. Being a dense prediction task, it requires a model to carry spatial detail through to the output rather than collapse the image into a vector.

Two models are compared under one split and one evaluation protocol: a U-Net written from scratch, and a pretrained DeepLabV3 fine-tuned on this data. The question is how much of the gap between them comes from pretrained representations and how much from architecture.

## Dataset and EDA

Cityscapes fine annotations: 5,000 frames at $1024 \times 2048$ recorded from a vehicle across 27 cities in Germany, France and Switzerland, annotated as per-pixel polygons. The dataset defines 34 labels and scores 19 of them; the rest, including void regions, the ego vehicle and the image border, carry an ignore label excluded from loss and metrics. They are $11.8\%$ of every frame. :ref[fig:samples] shows a frame and its annotation.

:::figure{label="fig:samples"}
![Three rows, each pairing a street scene photographed from a vehicle with the same scene under its colour-coded per-pixel annotation](/DL261-Assignment/assignments/assignment-2/samples.png)

Three training frames and their label maps. The dark regions carry the ignore label: the bonnet of the recording vehicle along the bottom of every frame, and the rectification border at its edges.
:::

The partitions share no city, which is the property the comparison rests on: frames from one street cannot appear on both sides, so a model cannot score well by recognising a scene it has already trained on. The official test labels are withheld, so the official validation partition is reported here and the three cities that select a checkpoint are held out of training instead, as :ref[tab:splits] sets out.

:::table{label="tab:splits"}
| Partition | Cities | Images |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| Train | 15 | 2,406 |
| Validation | 3 | 569 |
| Test | 3 | 500 |

Split sizes.
:::

The class distribution decides which metric is meaningful. Road, building and vegetation occupy $75.8\%$ of evaluated pixels, so predicting nothing but those three already reaches roughly $0.76$ pixel accuracy. Mean intersection over union averages across classes instead, which is why it is primary. :ref[fig:classes] shows the distribution, measured over all 2,406 training label maps rather than a sample.

:::figure{label="fig:classes"}
![Two stacked bar charts over the same nineteen classes: above, the share of evaluated pixels on a logarithmic axis descending from road at 37 percent to motorcycle at 0.1 percent; below, the percentage of whole images and of 512-pixel crops in which each class appears](/DL261-Assignment/assignments/assignment-2/class_distribution.svg)

Pixel share and frequency per class. The upper axis is logarithmic because the classes span almost three orders of magnitude.
:::

Scarcity takes two forms that fail for different reasons. Poles occupy $1.17\%$ of pixels yet appear in $99\%$ of images and traffic signs $0.56\%$ in $94\%$: thin structures lost to boundary imprecision, not to a shortage of examples. Trains occupy $0.21\%$ in $4\%$ of images and buses $0.22\%$ in $9\%$, yet cover $0.91\%$ and $0.66\%$ of a frame when visible: large but rarely seen. A per-class table is reported alongside the mean, which alone cannot distinguish the two.

Training on random crops sharpens the second form. A $512 \times 512$ window covers an eighth of the frame, so trains fall to $2\%$ of crops and buses to $4\%$. Sky is the clearest case, present in $90\%$ of images but $25\%$ of crops, because it occupies a band at the top. Cropping reshapes the class prior spatially, not only by area.

## Methodology

:::figure{label="fig:pipeline"}
![Pipeline diagram in three rows: raw data, preprocessing and data loader feed the model; the model feeds loss, optimization and prediction; post-processing, evaluation and checkpointing feed the reporting stage, which averages three seeds and applies the decision rule](/DL261-Assignment/assignments/assignment-2/pipeline.svg)

From the raw archives to a reported number. Every arm takes this path; one block changes at a time.
:::

:ref[fig:pipeline] shows the path from the raw archives to a reported number. Post-processing is empty by design: both models emit logits at the input resolution, so the prediction is an `argmax` and nothing is upsampled, smoothed or thresholded afterwards. Evaluation runs on two different terms: validation every epoch selects the checkpoint, and the test split is read once, after selection has concluded.

Both models map a $3 \times 512 \times 512$ crop to per-pixel logits over the 19 evaluated classes, and both are trained with the same loss, optimiser and schedule. What differs is where their features come from: one learns everything from the 2,406 training frames, the other starts from weights fitted on a far larger corpus.

### U-Net, the baseline

A symmetric encoder and decoder written from scratch, base width 32 and depth 4, with no pretrained weights. The encoder applies two padded $3 \times 3$ convolutions at each resolution, each normalised and rectified, then halves the resolution by max pooling; widths double at every level, $32 \to 64 \to 128 \to 256$, and a $512$-wide block bridges the bottom. The decoder mirrors that, upsampling by transposed convolution and ending in a $1 \times 1$ convolution to the 19 logits.

The skip connections are the point of the architecture rather than a detail of it. Four poolings reduce a 512-pixel crop to 32 pixels, which carries enough context to say what a region is but is far too coarse to say where its boundary runs. Each decoder stage therefore concatenates the encoder map of its own resolution, placing the upsampled semantics back onto the detail that produced them. Padding keeps every convolution at its input size, so a skip always meets a map of exactly matching resolution.

Convolutions carry no bias, because each is followed by batch normalisation, which recentres the activations and makes the bias redundant. The model holds $7{,}763{,}635$ trainable parameters.

### DeepLabV3, the pretrained model

Torchvision's DeepLabV3 with a MobileNetV3-Large backbone pretrained on COCO with VOC labels, $11{,}024{,}963$ trainable parameters. The published weights predict 21 classes, so the final $1 \times 1$ convolution is replaced with a 19-class one; the backbone and the atrous spatial pyramid pooling module transfer unchanged, since they are the parts that carry the learned features.

Two details matter for the comparison. The auxiliary head the architecture can carry is removed rather than computed and discarded, so training pays nothing for an output no loss reads. And the preprocessing is taken from the weights themselves rather than assumed: feeding a differently normalised batch to a pretrained backbone degrades it silently, with no error to notice.

### What the two share

Both consume the identical committed split and the identical preprocessing, so a difference between them is attributable to architecture and pretraining rather than to data handling. At base width 32 the baseline holds $7.8$M parameters against the pretrained model's $11.0$M, close enough that capacity is not the obvious explanation for any gap.

## Experimental setup

Every value in :ref[tab:protocol] is identical for both models. A run that deviates from it is repeated rather than reported.

:::table{label="tab:protocol"}
| Setting | Value |
| :--- | :--- |
| Input | resized to $512 \times 1024$; training takes a random $512 \times 512$ crop, evaluation the whole resized image |
| Normalization | ImageNet statistics, read from the pretrained weights, identical for both models |
| Augmentation | random crop, then horizontal flip with probability $0.5$, training loader only |
| Optimizer | AdamW, learning rate $3 \times 10^{-4}$, weight decay $0.05$ |
| Batch size | 8 |
| Schedule | cosine annealing over the configured epochs |
| Loss | cross-entropy with `ignore_index=255` |
| Checkpoint | the epoch with the highest validation mIoU |
| Early stopping | after 8 epochs without improvement |
| Hardware | Apple M4 Pro, 14 cores, 48 GB, MPS backend |

The shared training protocol.
:::

**Leakage controls.** Three, each closing a different route. The split indices are generated once and committed, so every model on every machine reads identical data. Normalization statistics never come from the data a model is scored on. And the test partition, which is the official Cityscapes validation set because the official test labels are withheld, is read once per configuration after model selection concludes, so it never influences a decision.

**Metrics.** Mean intersection over union is primary, with Dice and per-class IoU reported beside it. Pixel accuracy is recorded but never decides a comparison: road, building and vegetation occupy $75.8\%$ of evaluated pixels, so predicting nothing but those three already reaches roughly $0.76$. Pixels carrying the ignore label are excluded from both the loss and every metric.

**Decision rule.** Fixed before any result was seen. One configuration is better than another only when its mean validation mIoU is higher *and* the two intervals of mean plus or minus one standard deviation do not overlap; otherwise the two are reported as indistinguishable, which is itself a result. A claim about a single class applies the same rule to that class's IoU. A configuration run at one seed supports no claim of superiority and is reported as indicative.

**Search effort.** No hyperparameter search is run. Both models use the shared protocol and their committed configuration, and an experiment changes exactly one key, so the search effort is zero and identical across arms.

## Results

Thirteen runs: three seeds each for the baseline, the pretrained model, the controlled experiment and the ablation, plus one extension run. 25.5 hours of training in total on one machine, run consecutively so the timings compare with each other.

| Configuration | Parameters | s/epoch | Hours | ms/image | Peak GB | Validation mIoU | Test mIoU |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| DeepLabV3-ResNet50 *(1 seed)* | 39,638,355 | 739.9 | 8.22 | 72.3 | 4.37 | *0.6665* | *0.7076* |
| **DeepLabV3-MobileNetV3** | 11,024,963 | 140.4 | 1.52 | 15.2 | 3.41 | **0.6136 ± 0.0051** | **0.6180 ± 0.0049** |
| *frozen backbone* | 8,053,011 | 95.3 | 0.84 | 14.7 | 3.41 | 0.4961 ± 0.0049 | 0.5003 ± 0.0088 |
| *Dice loss* | 11,024,963 | 150.5 | 0.50 | 14.9 | 3.41 | 0.4958 ± 0.0045 | 0.4983 ± 0.0123 |
| U-Net, from scratch | 7,763,635 | 260.2 | 2.89 | 22.6 | 6.57 | 0.4836 ± 0.0154 | 0.4702 ± 0.0071 |

:::

Validation mIoU is the quantity the decision rule is applied to, because it selected the checkpoints; the test column was read once, afterwards, and decides nothing. The evaluation reproduces the training-time validation mIoU exactly for all thirteen runs, which confirms the checkpoint written during training is the one scored here.

Three things in :ref[tab:results] are worth stating before any comparison.

**Pixel accuracy ranks the models in the opposite order.** The U-Net reaches $0.9202$ pixel accuracy against the frozen arm's $0.8959$ and the Dice arm's $0.8982$, while scoring lower than both on mIoU. A metric dominated by the three classes covering $75.8\%$ of the ground disagrees with the metric that weights all nineteen equally. This is the measured justification for the choice made in the EDA, and it is why pixel accuracy appears here only as a secondary number.

**Validation does not overstate test; for four arms of five it understates it.** Test mIoU exceeds validation by $0.0043$ for DeepLabV3, $0.0042$ frozen, $0.0024$ Dice and $0.0412$ for ResNet50. Only the U-Net is lower on test, by $0.0134$. With a city-level split the three test cities are a different sample of difficulty from the three validation cities, and that partition-to-partition difference is of the same order as the seed-to-seed spread. What transfers between the two columns is the margin between arms, not the absolute level.

**The seed spread is small except for the U-Net.** Its standard deviation is $0.0154$, three times that of the pretrained arms, with individual seeds at $0.4687$, $0.4824$ and $0.4995$. Its best seed alone would have been indistinguishable from the frozen arm; its worst would have lost under the rule. A single-seed study could have reported either outcome, which is the concrete argument for running three.

:::figure{label="fig:curves"}
![Two panels sharing an epoch axis: training objective on the left and mean IoU on the right, one line per configuration averaged over three seeds, solid for validation and dashed for training, each with a shaded band; curves end at different epochs where early stopping fired](/DL261-Assignment/assignments/assignment-2/learning_curves.svg)

Learning curves per configuration, averaged over seeds. The left axis mixes two objectives and should be read only within an arm.
:::

:ref[fig:curves] shows that the 40-epoch budget was the right size for DeepLabV3, whose best epochs were 30, 31 and 35 of 38, 39 and 40 and whose validation curve is flat from about epoch 32. The U-Net is the exception: its best epochs were 38, 40 and 39 of 40 and its validation mIoU rises monotonically to the last epoch, $0.446$ at 28, $0.460$ at 32, $0.466$ at 36, $0.468$ at 40. **It was still improving when the budget ran out, so $0.4836$ is a floor rather than a converged score.**

Overfitting is present but mild and is not what separates the arms. The gap between training and validation mIoU at the selected epoch is $0.121$ for DeepLabV3, $0.110$ frozen, $0.165$ for ResNet50 and only $0.021$ for the U-Net, which is consistent with a model still underfitting. The Dice arm's gap is $-0.002$: it does not overfit at all, it simply stops improving.

## Comparison and discussion

:::table{label="tab:verdicts"}
| Comparison | Verdict | Margin |
| :--- | :--- | ---: |
| DeepLabV3 vs. U-Net | DeepLabV3 better | 0.1301 |
| DeepLabV3 vs. frozen backbone | Full fine-tuning better | 0.1175 |
| DeepLabV3 vs. Dice loss | Cross-entropy better | 0.1178 |
| U-Net vs. frozen backbone | **Indistinguishable** | 0.0126 |
| U-Net vs. Dice loss | **Indistinguishable** | 0.0123 |
| Frozen backbone vs. Dice loss | **Indistinguishable** | 0.0003 |
| Anything vs. ResNet50 | Indicative only, one seed | n/a |

Every pairwise comparison under the pre-registered decision rule, on validation mIoU.
:::

**One large effect, and three ways of losing it.** Only the fully fine-tuned pretrained model separates from anything. The other three three-seed arms are mutually indistinguishable, clustered within $0.0126$ validation mIoU despite differing in architecture, in pretraining and in objective; the frozen and Dice arms differ by $0.0003$. Freezing the backbone, swapping cross-entropy for Dice, and training a different architecture from scratch are three unrelated interventions, and all three land in the same place. On this dataset at this budget, what matters is not which of several reasonable choices is made but whether the model starts from adapted pretrained features at all.

:::figure{label="fig:per-class"}
![Grouped bar chart of per-class IoU for the five configurations over the nineteen classes, ordered from hardest at the left to easiest at the right; ResNet50 leads on almost every class and the frozen arm trails on all of them](/DL261-Assignment/assignments/assignment-2/per_class_iou_test.svg)

Per-class IoU on the test split, classes ordered from hardest to easiest.
:::

**Where the difference appears.** Against the frozen arm, full fine-tuning is better on *all nineteen* classes with non-overlapping intervals, so nothing about the frozen arm is preserved. But the gap is very unevenly distributed: $0.027$ on road, $0.044$ on car and $0.048$ on building, against $0.215$ on truck, $0.213$ on traffic sign, $0.173$ on traffic light and $0.166$ on bus. A frozen object-centric backbone still supports the classes that fill a street scene and loses precisely the classes that do not.

This partly refutes the pre-registered hypothesis, which predicted the gap would be widest on *thin* classes. It is right for traffic sign and traffic light, second and third largest, but the single widest gap is truck, a rare-but-large class, and pole, the thin class named explicitly, sits mid-table at $0.128$. The correct statement is narrower: freezing costs most on classes that are rare in the training distribution, whether rare because thin in area or because seldom photographed.

**Pretraining is not uniformly better, which the aggregate hides.** The from-scratch U-Net beats the pretrained DeepLabV3, with non-overlapping intervals, on three classes: pole ($0.4126$ against $0.3277$), sky ($0.9332$ against $0.9211$) and vegetation ($0.8823$ against $0.8740$). Pole is the interesting one, a $26\%$ relative margin for the weaker model. The cause is architectural rather than about features: DeepLabV3 never resolves below a stride of $8$ and returns its logits to full resolution by one bilinear interpolation, which cannot reconstruct a structure a few pixels wide, whereas the U-Net's skip connections carry full-resolution detail into the decoder. The from-scratch baseline is better at localisation and far worse at recognition, and mIoU averages the two into a number that reflects only the second.

**Compute cost.** The best accuracy-per-cost model is also the most accurate of the three-seed arms. The U-Net is dominated on every axis at once: $30\%$ *fewer* parameters than DeepLabV3 yet $1.9\times$ the seconds per epoch, $1.5\times$ the latency at batch 1, $2.7\times$ at batch 8, $1.9\times$ the peak memory, and $0.130$ lower mIoU. Parameter count is not a proxy for cost here, because the U-Net's convolutions run at full resolution and its decoder holds every encoder map alive for the skip connections. The frozen arm is the one genuine trade: $0.84$ hours against $1.52$, a $45\%$ saving, for $0.118$ mIoU. That is smaller than "freezing the backbone" suggests, since $73\%$ of parameters remain trainable, and it is a saving in *training* only, as inference runs the same forward pass either way ($14.7$ against $15.2$ ms).

**Scaling the backbone works, and is expensive.** ResNet50 reaches $0.6665$ validation and $0.7076$ test mIoU. At one seed this supports no claim of superiority, and it is reported as indicative; what can be said is that the $0.0529$ validation margin is ten times the seed-to-seed standard deviation measured for the same architecture under the same protocol, so it is unlikely to be seed noise alone. The cost is $5.3\times$ the seconds per epoch, $4.8\times$ the single-image latency and $3.6\times$ the parameters for roughly a $9\%$ relative gain. One ResNet50 run costs more than the nine MobileNetV3 runs combined.

**What does not follow.** The U-Net's $0.4836$ is a floor, so "pretraining beats from-scratch by $0.130$" is an upper bound on that margin rather than an estimate of it. The model comparison's arms differ in architecture as well as in pretraining, and no run here separates a DeepLabV3 trained from scratch from a pretrained one. And every number is a score at an untuned configuration under one shared protocol, so none is the best the corresponding method could reach.

Most of the courtyard carries the ignore label, so little of the frame is scored and the few classes present have small support, and a per-image mean over classes is extremely sensitive when each class is a handful of pixels. ## Error analysis

DeepLabV3 mislabels $15.37$M of the $229.2$M labelled test pixels, an error rate of $6.71\%$ averaged over three seeds. :ref[tab:errors] partitions every error pixel into six groups by the pair of classes involved, with precedence top to bottom so each error falls in exactly one group.

:::table{label="tab:errors"}
| Group | DeepLabV3 | ResNet50 | Frozen | Dice | U-Net |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Thin structures | 24.7% | 23.0% | 19.6% | 21.9% | 18.7% |
| Upright surfaces | 24.0% | 26.5% | 24.2% | 24.0% | 23.4% |
| Participant vs. background | 17.5% | 15.7% | 16.5% | 17.7% | 15.8% |
| Ground surfaces | 14.6% | 14.8% | 20.3% | 17.4% | 17.1% |
| Traffic participants | 6.5% | 6.8% | 6.3% | 6.4% | **12.5%** |
| Other | 12.7% | 13.3% | 13.1% | 12.7% | 12.4% |
| **Error rate over all labelled pixels** | 6.7% | **4.9%** | 10.4% | 10.2% | 8.0% |

Each cell is the share of that model's own error pixels.
:::

**The structure of the errors is a property of the task, not of the model.** The five configurations differ by more than a factor of two in error rate, from $4.9\%$ to $10.4\%$, yet their error *composition* is nearly identical: thin structures take between $19$ and $25\%$ for all five, upright surfaces between $23$ and $27\%$, ground surfaces between $15$ and $20\%$. Improving the model lowers the total and leaves the proportions where they were. There is one exception, the fourth group below.

**Thin structures, $24.7\%$.** Any error with a pole, traffic light or traffic sign on either side. The largest single flows in the whole matrix sit here: pole predicted as building, $0.91$M pixels or $5.9\%$ of all errors, and pole as vegetation, $0.56$M or $3.6\%$. Pole is the hardest class of the nineteen at $0.3230$ IoU *despite appearing in $99\%$ of images*, so this is not data scarcity but a resolution failure: a stride-8 feature map upsampled bilinearly cannot represent a structure a few pixels wide. The evidence that this is architectural is that the from-scratch U-Net, with full-resolution skip connections, scores $0.4361$ on pole against DeepLabV3's $0.3230$ while losing on almost everything else.

**Upright surfaces, $24.0\%$.** Confusions among building, wall, fence and vegetation, led by building predicted as vegetation, $1.16$M pixels, and vegetation as building, $1.13$M. The near-symmetry is the signature of a boundary error rather than a systematic bias: these are overwhelmingly pixels where a tree overhangs a facade. Wall and fence add a second cause, being low vertical barriers separated from building by a taxonomy convention rather than by appearance, and both are among the weakest classes at $0.4345$ and $0.4689$.

**Ground surfaces, $14.6\%$.** Road, sidewalk and terrain confused with one another, $1.12$M pixels of sidewalk predicted as road and $0.74$M the reverse. The kerb between road and sidewalk is a few pixels high and often occluded by parked cars. This group grows to $20.3\%$ for the frozen arm, the largest compositional shift in the table.

**Traffic participants, $6.5\%$, and the one place a model differs in kind.** This is the smallest group for every DeepLabV3 arm, at between $6.3$ and $6.8\%$, and $12.5\%$ for the U-Net, nearly double. The per-class table shows why: the U-Net scores $0.0205$ on rider against DeepLabV3's $0.4316$, and $0.0108$ on motorcycle. Rider and person differ not in appearance but in what the person is sitting on, which requires recognising the vehicle beneath them, and the from-scratch model never learns it. This is the one error group where the deficit is a failure of recognition rather than of localisation, and it is where pretrained features help most.

**Participant versus background, $17.5\%$, and other, $12.7\%$.** The first is a person or vehicle confused with scenery, led by person predicted as building, $0.22$M, and road as car, $0.21$M, which are object boundaries and shadow regions. The remainder is thinly spread and dominated by vegetation against terrain, $0.25$M, and building against sky, $0.22$M, again boundary pairs.

**What would improve it.** Five of the six groups are dominated by boundary pixels, which points at resolution rather than at the objective or the data. In descending order of expected return: evaluating and training at the native $1024 \times 2048$ would halve the boundary length measured in pixels, and the resize to $512 \times 1024$ was a choice made for compute, not accuracy; an architecture keeping a full-resolution path, which the U-Net's advantage on pole suggests concretely, would address the largest group; and only the traffic-participant group, the smallest, would benefit from more or better data. What is *not* indicated is a reweighted loss, which the ablation measures directly.

## Ablation study

The ablation replaces cross-entropy with soft Dice, holding the model, the data and every other protocol value fixed. The hypothesis, fixed in advance, was that Dice would raise IoU on train, bus and truck, which are rare but large when present, because it weights classes rather than pixels, and would not help pole or traffic sign, whose errors are boundaries. The pre-registered criterion was that the hypothesis holds only if the per-class rule separates the arms on at least two of train, bus and truck.

:::table{label="tab:ablation"}
| Class | Cross-entropy | Dice | Change | P(absent from batch) |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| train | 0.6007 ± 0.0383 | **0.0000 ± 0.0000** | −0.6007 | 85.1% |
| bus | 0.4154 ± 0.0923 | 0.1944 ± 0.1696 | −0.2211 | 70.0% |
| truck | 0.3797 ± 0.0398 | 0.1952 ± 0.0233 | −0.1845 | 69.3% |
| motorcycle | 0.4661 ± 0.0064 | 0.2775 ± 0.0313 | −0.1886 | 60.2% |
| traffic sign | 0.5518 ± 0.0036 | 0.4692 ± 0.0068 | −0.0826 | 0.6% |
| pole | 0.3277 ± 0.0085 | 0.3048 ± 0.0072 | −0.0229 | 0.0% |
| road | 0.9722 ± 0.0004 | 0.9502 ± 0.0016 | −0.0220 | 0.0% |

Validation IoU, mean ± one standard deviation over three seeds. The last column is the estimated probability that a batch of eight $512 \times 512$ crops contains no pixel of that class.
:::

**The hypothesis is refuted, and in the opposite direction.** The arms are separated on train and on truck, so the criterion is met in form, but Dice is *worse* on both, and worse on all nineteen classes individually, and worse overall at $0.4958 \pm 0.0045$ against $0.6136 \pm 0.0051$.

**The mechanism is measurable.** Across all nineteen classes, the correlation between the probability that a class is absent from a training batch and the damage Dice does to that class is $r = -0.809$. The four worst-damaged classes are exactly the four most often absent.

The cause lies in how the two objectives treat a class with no positive pixels in the batch. Under cross-entropy such a class contributes nothing of its own; it is only a competitor in the softmax denominator. Under Dice it contributes a full term, $(0 + \varepsilon) / (\sum_\Omega p_{cij} + 0 + \varepsilon)$, which is maximised by driving that class's predicted probability to zero everywhere in the batch. **Dice actively suppresses every class that happens to be missing from the current eight crops.** Measured over sampled batches, $10.43$ of the $19$ classes appear in a given $512 \times 512$ crop and $15.92$ in a batch of eight, so $16.2\%$ of the Dice terms in a typical step are suppression terms. For train, present in $2\%$ of crops, roughly $85\%$ of the $301$ steps in an epoch push its logit down and about $45$ push it up. The result is the only exact zero in this report: under Dice the model never predicts a single train pixel, on any seed, on either split.

This also explains the training behaviour. The Dice arm peaked at epoch 4 on all three seeds and early stopping ended it at epoch 12, with a training-to-validation gap of $-0.002$. It is not overfitting and not underfitting in the usual sense: the objective reaches a state where the suppression terms balance the overlap terms and stops producing useful gradient. It is also the only arm more expensive per epoch than its cross-entropy counterpart, $150.5$ against $140.4$ seconds, and is cheapest overall only because it quits early.

**What this does and does not establish.** It establishes that an unweighted multi-class soft Dice with $\varepsilon = 1$, computed per batch at batch size 8, is the wrong objective for this class distribution, and identifies the reason precisely enough to act on. It does not establish that class-balanced objectives are useless here: accumulating Dice over an epoch rather than a batch, masking absent classes out of the mean, or a class-weighted cross-entropy that adds no suppression term all avoid the specific mechanism measured above, and none of them was run.

## Limitations and conclusion

Six limits bound these results. **The U-Net is undertrained**, with validation mIoU still rising at epoch 40 on all three seeds, so $0.4836$ is a floor and the margin over it an upper bound. **The model comparison confounds two factors**, since its arms differ in architecture as well as in pretraining. **The backbone extension is one seed** and supports no claim of superiority. **Three seeds estimate a standard deviation from three observations**, so "indistinguishable" means not separated at this sample size, not equal. **The data is narrow**: daylight, fair weather, European cities, one camera, and the images were resized to a quarter of their pixels for compute, itself a ceiling on the thin classes that dominate the error analysis. **And reproducibility has a hardware limit**: MPS provides no deterministic kernel for the interpolation DeepLabV3 uses, so rerunning a DeepLabV3 seed will not reproduce its number exactly, and the reported spread includes that nondeterminism.

The research question was how much of the gap between a from-scratch model and a pretrained one comes from pretrained features rather than from architecture, loss or capacity. The evidence gives a sharp answer and an unexpected qualification. Fully fine-tuned pretrained features are worth $0.1301$ validation mIoU over the from-scratch baseline, $0.1175$ over the same weights with the backbone frozen, and $0.1178$ over the same model trained with a class-balanced objective; and those three alternatives are mutually indistinguishable, within $0.0126$ of one another. One factor accounts for essentially the whole spread. The qualification is that the advantage is not uniform: the from-scratch U-Net is better, with separated intervals, on pole, sky and vegetation, because a full-resolution decoder localises thin structures that a stride-8 backbone cannot, and five of the six error groups are dominated by boundary pixels. **Pretraining supplies recognition rather than resolution.**

DeepLabV3-MobileNetV3, fully fine-tuned, is carried forward at **0.6180 ± 0.0049** test mIoU and $0.7441 \pm 0.0048$ test Dice, at $140$ seconds per epoch and $15.2$ ms per image.

Three directions follow, in the order the evidence supports them. Resolution first: training and evaluating at the native $1024 \times 2048$, or adding a decoder with a full-resolution path, attacks the thin-structure and boundary groups that are most of the error. Second, the clean version of the model comparison, a DeepLabV3 trained from scratch, would separate pretraining from architecture in a single run. Third, the class-balancing question the ablation reopened rather than closed: an epoch-accumulated Dice, or one that masks absent classes out of its mean, avoids the suppression mechanism measured here.
